01 · Start with the map

Three ideas. One connected education system.

The easiest way to remember the relationship is to see each framework as answering a different question.

Memory shortcut: Vision → Design → Evidence
01
The vision

NEP 2020

What kind of education should India build? It sets the direction: equitable, flexible, holistic, multidisciplinary and competency-focused learning.

02
The learning design

NCF

How should that vision enter the classroom? It guides curriculum, pedagogy, learning experiences and assessment by developmental stage.

03
The progress picture

HPC

How do we understand a learner's growth? It brings together evidence of academic, social, emotional, physical, ethical and creative development.

NEP sets the direction · NCF shapes the journey · HPC helps us notice progress.

Keep this distinction clear: NEP is a national policy, NCF is a curriculum framework, and HPC is an assessment and reporting approach. They are connected, but they are not interchangeable documents.
02 · The big comparison

Before NEP 2020 and the direction after it

The table compares the dominant patterns that the reform seeks to change with the direction the policy encourages. It is not a claim that every pre-2020 school taught in the same way, or that every school has changed completely since 2020.

ThemeEarlier dominant pattern / challengeDirection encouraged by NEP 2020 + NCF
School stages10+2 was the familiar curricular structure, with pre-primary often treated separately from the school continuum.A 5+3+3+4 curricular and pedagogical design covers ages 3–18, bringing early childhood education into the framework.
Classroom learningIn many settings, pressure to finish the syllabus and prepare for exams encouraged teacher-talk, textbook coverage and memorisation.More play, discovery, discussion, experimentation, projects, reflection and application of concepts.
Early childhoodPre-primary provision varied widely; the transition into Grade 1 could be abrupt.ECCE and school readiness are treated as the foundation, with play-based learning for ages 3–8.
Subjects & skillsAcademic, vocational, arts and extracurricular pathways could feel separated or unequal in status.Less rigid separation; arts, sports, vocational exposure and interdisciplinary learning should sit within a broader education.
AssessmentHigh-stakes written tests and marks often dominated how achievement was communicated.More formative and competency-based assessment, with feedback on what a child understands and can apply.
Progress reportsA report card often summarised subject marks, grades, attendance and teacher remarks.HPC aims for a wider view of growth, learning transitions, strengths and needs across multiple domains.
TechnologyDigital learning existed, but access and classroom integration were uneven; the pandemic exposed the access gap.Digital infrastructure, blended learning, digital literacy and responsible use of emerging technologies receive greater attention.
Work & life skillsVocational learning, internships and practical problem-solving were not consistently integrated across school experience.Early vocational exposure, internships from the middle stage, critical thinking, communication and problem-solving are encouraged.
Important nuance: 5+3+3+4 is a curricular and pedagogical structure, not a magic administrative switch that instantly changed every school building, board examination or class label. Schools, boards and States implement reforms at different speeds. The old patterns also did not disappear on the day NEP was approved.
03 · Understand the structure

The 5+3+3+4 model, made visual

The stages are designed around how children develop. The first five years include three years of preschool / Anganwadi / Balvatika and Grades 1–2.

Ages 11–14 · Subjects and projects

Ages 14–18 · Depth and choice
🧸

Foundational · Learn through play

Build language, early numeracy, motor skills, curiosity and social confidence through stories, songs, blocks, conversation and movement.

🔎

Preparatory · Discover patterns

Build strong reading, writing and numeracy. Introduce science, mathematics and the world through hands-on tasks, observation and guided exploration.

🧪

Middle · Connect subjects to life

Introduce more abstract ideas and subject depth while retaining projects, experiments, coding, vocational exposure and practical problem-solving.

🧭

Secondary · Go deeper and choose

Develop analysis, independence, subject depth and flexibility. Connect learning to interests, life aspirations, careers and multidisciplinary pathways.

Policy reference: NEP 2020, Chapter 4, especially paragraphs 4.1–4.2. The age bands are approximate stage guidance.

04 · Six years in the real world

What happened from 2020 to 2026?

Follow the path from policy approval to curriculum design, school programmes, assessment systems and the arrival of structured AI learning.

Timeline through 20 November 2026
2020

The policy moment

17 MAY 2020PM e-VIDYA launched during the COVID-19 disruption

Multi-mode learning through digital platforms, TV, radio and other channels helped keep learning available when schools were closed. Timeline nuance: this initiative began before NEP was approved, so it should not be described as caused by NEP.

29 JUL 2020NEP 2020 approved by the Union Cabinet

Introduced the 5+3+3+4 vision, stronger focus on foundational learning, holistic education, flexibility, vocational exposure and assessment reform.

2021

Foundations become a mission

8 APR 2021SARTHAQ implementation plan released

A roadmap to support implementation of NEP recommendations for students and teachers.

5 JUL 2021NIPUN Bharat launched

A national mission to strengthen foundational literacy and numeracy, with the stated goal that children achieve foundational skills by Grade 3 by 2026–27.

29 JUL 2021Vidya Pravesh introduced

A three-month, play-based school preparation module for children entering Grade 1.

2022

New learning frameworks take shape

7 SEP 2022PM SHRI scheme launched

Selected existing schools are to be strengthened as exemplar schools aligned with NEP, including pedagogy, inclusion, infrastructure and sustainability.

20 OCT 2022NCF for Foundational Stage (NCF-FS) released

Guidance for curriculum and pedagogy for ages 3–8, with play-based and developmentally appropriate learning at the centre.

2023

Frameworks reach the classroom toolkit

8 FEB 2023PARAKH established within NCERT

National assessment centre to develop norms and support competency-based assessment, large-scale surveys, board equivalence and holistic progress cards.

20 FEB 2023Jaadui Pitara launched

A play-based learning-teaching collection for children aged 3–8, with toys, puzzles, books, activity cards and other resources.

23 AUG 2023NCF for School Education (NCF-SE) released

Translates the NEP vision into curricular areas, stage-wise learning, pedagogy and assessment guidance for school education.

2024

Student records and system-level evidence

ACADEMIC YEAR 2024–25NCERT introduces new Grade 3 and Grade 6 textbooks

New learning materials aligned with NCF-SE began a phased transition, including expanded attention to arts, physical education and vocational learning.

7 FEB 2024APAAR rollout through Common Service Centres

“One Nation, One Student ID” was designed as a lifelong academic identity and record pathway. Consent, data protection and correct record management matter.

DEC 2024PARAKH Rashtriya Sarvekshan 2024 conducted

Large-scale assessment of competencies in Grades 3, 6 and 9. More than 21.15 lakh students from over 74,000 schools in 781 districts participated.

IMPORTANT CONTEXTAI in CBSE did not begin with NEP

CBSE introduced AI as an optional skill module / subject in 2019–20, before NEP. Later AI and computational-thinking reforms build on an evolving landscape.

2025

Early signals and AI preparation

4 FEB 2025ASER 2024 findings published

Rural Grade 3 government-school children able to read a Grade 2 text rose from 16.3% in 2022 to 23.4% in 2024. This is encouraging recovery, not proof that one policy alone caused it.

30 OCT 2025National consultation on AI & Computational Thinking

Ministry announced work with CBSE, NCERT, KVS, NVS and States on an age-appropriate AI and computational-thinking curriculum, envisaged from Grade 3.

2026

AI moves into the school curriculum

1 APR 2026CBSE CT & AI curriculum launched for Classes III–VIII

For 2026–27, the curriculum builds logical reasoning, pattern recognition, problem-solving, digital literacy and responsible AI awareness.

27 JUL 2026PM SHRI network update

13,092 schools had been selected nationally against a target of more than 14,500. Telangana had 832 selected schools in the published state table.

2026 EVIDENCEPARAKH results and system reviews inform the next phase

Government reporting notes improvements against NAS 2021 in foundational competencies. NITI Aayog's 2026 review also identifies persistent gaps in classroom practice, equity and digital access.

How to read a timeline: “Launched”, “released”, “selected” and “implemented” are not the same as “fully completed everywhere”. School education is shared across the Union and States, and the pace differs across boards, districts and schools.

06 · Know the three frameworks deeply

NEP, NCF and HPC in practical language

Use these cards as your revision notes. The most important test is whether you can translate each idea into something a teacher or school can actually do.

POLICY · THE WHY
🧭

NEP 2020

A national policy approved on 29 July 2020. It sets the long-term direction for school and higher education.

  • Access, equity, quality, affordability and accountability.
  • Early childhood care and education.
  • Foundational literacy and numeracy.
  • Multilingualism and Indian knowledge systems.
  • Experiential, flexible and multidisciplinary learning.
  • Vocational exposure and assessment reform.
CURRICULUM · THE HOW
🧩

NCF

A national curriculum framework developed by NCERT to translate the policy vision into educational design.

  • NCF-FS 2022: ages 3–8.
  • NCF-SE 2023: school education.
  • Stage-wise learning goals and competencies.
  • Pedagogy that fits developmental stages.
  • Curricular areas, textbooks and learning resources.
  • Guidance for assessment and classroom practice.
ASSESSMENT · THE EVIDENCE
🌿

HPC

The Holistic Progress Card aims to record learning transitions and the whole learner, rather than reducing progress to marks alone.

  • Tracks cognitive development.
  • Includes social and emotional growth.
  • Looks at physical and creative development.
  • Includes ethical development and values.
  • Uses observation and multiple forms of evidence.
  • Helps plan the next learning step.

What can a Holistic Progress Card notice?

PARAKH describes HPC as a 360-degree approach across learning domains. The examples below are classroom-friendly ways to observe those areas; a teacher should use the stage-specific HPC format and school/board guidance.

🧠 CognitiveReasoning, remembering, questioning, applying concepts.
💬 Language & communicationListening, speaking, reading, writing and expressing ideas.
🤝 SocialCooperation, empathy, teamwork and respect.
💛 EmotionalSelf-awareness, confidence, resilience and emotion regulation.
🏃 PhysicalMovement, coordination, health habits and participation.
🎨 Creative & ethicalImagination, original work, responsibility and thoughtful choices.
Do not confuse HPC with “no exams”. Assessment still happens. The shift is towards a richer body of evidence: what the child knows, can do, explain, create and improve over time.
07 · See the difference in action

Same topic. Two very different learning experiences.

These are illustrative lesson designs, not claims that every classroom used the older method. They help you practise converting a concept into an experiential, competency-focused lesson.

Example 1 · Science

Topic: Parts of a plant

Recall-led version: copy a labelled diagram, memorise terms and answer a short test.

Experiential version: observe a plant, sketch it, gently inspect roots and leaves, keep a growth journal and explain what each part does.

Competencies: observation, vocabulary, explanation, curiosity.

Example 2 · Mathematics

Topic: Addition and subtraction

Recall-led version: practise sums from the board and memorise the procedure.

Experiential version: use seeds, bottle caps or blocks to model quantities, create a small shop, calculate totals and explain the strategy.

Competencies: number sense, reasoning, application, communication.

Example 3 · Language

Topic: Story comprehension

Recall-led version: memorise the summary and answer textbook questions.

Experiential version: retell the story, role-play a character, create a new ending and explain why a character made a decision.

Competencies: listening, speaking, empathy, creativity.

Example 4 · Environmental studies

Topic: Water conservation

Recall-led version: write five slogans about saving water.

Experiential version: map water use at school, identify a leaking tap, estimate water saved, interview the caretaker and present a practical proposal.

Competencies: research, teamwork, measurement, civic responsibility.

Localise the lesson: Use examples children can see around them: a local market, a bus stop, a farm, a lake, a neighbourhood water source, a local language story or a community problem. Context makes concepts easier to understand and remember.

09 · From chalkboard to AI

How education technology evolved

AI did not appear from nowhere. Each generation of technology changed what teachers and learners could access, create and practise.

🪨
Before mass digital learning

Books, blackboards and broadcast

Printed textbooks, classroom explanation, libraries, radio and educational television formed the main learning environment for many students.

💻
1990s–2010s

Computers and the internet

Computer labs, search engines, multimedia, online courses, digital boards and learning-management systems expanded access to information.

📱
2020 pandemic period

Learning becomes multi-channel

Online classes, TV, radio, recorded lessons and mobile platforms became essential continuity tools. Access to devices and connectivity became a major equity issue.

🤖
2022 onward

Generative AI arrives

Tools can generate explanations, examples, images, code, translations and feedback. They can also produce confident errors and biased outputs.

🧠
2024–2025

AI literacy becomes a curriculum question

UNESCO published AI competency frameworks for students and teachers, emphasising human-centred thinking, ethics, application and design.

🌐
2026–27

Structured CT & AI learning

CBSE launched a Computational Thinking and AI curriculum for Classes III–VIII for the 2026–27 session, moving from occasional exposure towards structured learning.

A useful historical correction: CBSE had already introduced AI as an optional skill module / subject from 2019–20, before NEP 2020. The 2025–26 and 2026–27 developments represent a broader move towards age-appropriate computational thinking and AI education, not the first time an Indian school encountered AI.
10 · Present signals → future possibilities

What could education look like in an AI-shaped world?

These are reasoned possibilities, not guaranteed predictions. The strongest future system will combine technology with human judgement, relationships, practical experience and ethical responsibility.

✨ 1. Personalised practice

  • AI may give each learner additional examples at the right level.
  • Students could practise reading, maths or language at their own pace.
  • Teachers could use learning signals to identify who needs help.

Human role: decide whether the recommendation is suitable and emotionally supportive.

🗣️ 2. Multilingual learning support

  • Voice-based tools could explain concepts in local languages.
  • Translation can make resources more accessible.
  • Students could compare explanations and improve vocabulary.

Human role: check cultural context, accuracy and language quality.

🧪 3. More project-based evidence

  • Students may demonstrate learning through models, prototypes and field projects.
  • Oral explanation and reflection can reveal whether the student understands.
  • Portfolios can show improvement over time, not only one test day.

Human role: assess the learner's own reasoning, process and contribution.

👩‍🏫 4. Teachers as learning designers

  • AI may help draft lesson variations, quizzes and accessible materials.
  • Teachers may spend less time on repetitive preparation.
  • More time could go to mentoring, discussion, feedback and student wellbeing.

Human role: create belonging, set boundaries, motivate and make professional decisions.

The risks schools must design for

⚠️ Hallucinations

AI can invent facts, references or explanations. Students must cross-check claims.

🔐 Privacy

Do not upload children's personal data, photos, health details or school records to unapproved tools.

⚖️ Bias and exclusion

AI may perform poorly for some languages, accents, disability contexts or communities.

🧩 Over-dependence

Students may outsource thinking instead of learning how to frame, reason and solve problems.

📵 Unequal access

Devices, connectivity, electricity and adult support remain unevenly distributed.

🧍 Less human connection

Excessive screen time can crowd out conversation, movement, play and relationships.

Guiding principle: AI should extend human capability, not replace a child's thinking or a teacher's responsibility. A child should be able to explain, question and defend the work they submit.
11 · A practical AI lesson

How to teach AI without turning the class into a screen session

Example: Grade 6 students explore “How can our school reduce food waste?” The AI tool is one resource in a wider learning process.

Student learning journey

Ask → Observe → Compare → Create → Explain

  1. Ask students what food waste they notice in the school.
  2. Observe a lunch period and record anonymised counts or estimates.
  3. Ask an approved AI tool for possible causes and solutions.
  4. Compare AI suggestions with local evidence and teacher-approved sources.
  5. Create a poster, simple chart or proposal.
  6. Explain which suggestion they accepted or rejected, and why.
What the teacher assesses

AI output is not the student's learning

  • Can students frame a useful question?
  • Can they collect and interpret evidence?
  • Can they spot a weak or unsupported AI claim?
  • Can they explain their own reasoning?
  • Can they work respectfully in a team?
  • Can they reflect on ethical and practical impact?

The evidence is the student's thinking and work process, not just the polished answer.

Skills that become more valuable as AI improves

🧠 Critical thinkingAsk: Is this true? What evidence supports it?
🎯 Problem framingDefine the real problem before choosing a tool.
📊 Data literacyRead, compare and interpret information.
🧑‍🤝‍🧑 CommunicationExplain ideas, listen and collaborate.
🎨 CreativityImagine new approaches and make original work.
🛡️ Ethics & judgementProtect privacy, identify bias and take responsibility.
12 · The honest implementation picture

A policy can be ambitious. A classroom still needs support.

The 2026 NITI Aayog review is useful because it looks beyond announcements and examines learning outcomes, access, equity, infrastructure and classroom practice.

✅

What is moving forward

  • Curriculum frameworks for foundational and school stages are published.
  • NIPUN Bharat created a national focus on foundational literacy and numeracy.
  • PM SHRI is building a network of exemplar schools.
  • Assessment systems and large-scale competency measurement have expanded.
  • Digital infrastructure and AI curriculum work are advancing.
🛠️

What still needs work

  • Curriculum, teaching practice and assessment are not consistently aligned in classrooms.
  • Foundational learning gaps remain, particularly for children who need additional support.
  • State and district performance varies widely.
  • Digital access, teacher capacity and safe technology use need sustained attention.
  • Arts, life skills, wellbeing and parent engagement can be under-integrated.
What the evidence warns us: NITI Aayog's 2026 report notes that, in many schools, curriculum goals do not consistently translate into classroom practice; teaching can remain textbook-led and assessment can still emphasise recall. It also highlights digital divides, equity gaps and uneven implementation. Reform is a journey, not a switch.

14 · Clear up common confusion

Six myths worth correcting

Open each question to reveal the more accurate explanation.

“NEP 2020 is the same thing as NCF.”

No. NEP sets the policy vision. NCF gives curriculum and pedagogical guidance for putting that vision into practice.

“The 5+3+3+4 system changed every school overnight.”

No. It is the new curricular and pedagogical design. Adoption requires curriculum updates, textbooks, teacher development and State/board implementation over time.

“Mother-tongue teaching is compulsory in every school until Grade 8.”

The policy says the home, mother, local or regional language should be the medium of instruction wherever possible at least until Grade 5, preferably until Grade 8 and beyond. The wording and local implementation matter.

“HPC means examinations will disappear.”

No. HPC broadens the evidence of progress. Formal tests may still be used, but should not be the only way to understand learning.

“AI in Indian schools started after NEP.”

No. CBSE introduced AI as an optional skill module / subject in 2019–20. NEP and later curriculum work reinforce a broader future-skills direction.

“Any improvement after 2020 proves NEP caused it.”

No. Outcomes are influenced by many factors: pre-existing programmes, state initiatives, pandemic recovery, household conditions, teachers, funding, infrastructure and policy reforms. Use evidence carefully.
